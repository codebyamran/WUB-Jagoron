Booth 1

Check your digital privacy

What does the internet know about you? Open your own settings and mark each line. Nothing you tick leaves your phone.

  • My Facebook or Instagram profile is not public to strangers
  • Only people I choose can see my phone number
  • Only people I choose can see my email
  • Strangers cannot send me messages
  • My friend list is not public
  • Not everyone can tag me
  • Location sharing is off unless an app really needs it
  • I know which apps can use my location
  • I know which apps can use my camera and microphone
  • I only share what I'd put on a university notice board
Your score: mark each line to see it

0 to 2 Fix: Good3 to 5: Needs attention6 or more: High risk

Quick fixes

Facebook
Settings and privacy, then Privacy Checkup. Set profile details, posts and friend list to Friends or Only me.
Instagram
Settings and activity, then Account privacy, then turn on Private account.
WhatsApp
Settings, then Privacy. Set Last seen, Profile photo and About to My contacts.
Android
Settings, then Privacy, then Permission manager. Review Location, Camera and Microphone.
iPhone
Settings, then Privacy and Security. Review Location Services, Camera and Microphone.
Location rule
Choose "While using the app" for apps that need it, and "Don't allow" for the rest.

Menu names change with app updates and phone brands. Use the search bar in Settings.

If you wouldn't put it on a university notice board, think twice before putting it online.

Booth 2

Secure your device

If you lost your phone right now, could someone open it? Ten minutes is enough to:

  • Message your contacts"Brother, I'm in an emergency. Please send BDT 5,000 to this number."
  • Open your emailand reset your other accounts
  • See your private photosa serious privacy violation
  • Open payment appsand try unauthorised transactions
  • Change your settingsand lock you out
  • Post as youand damage your reputation
The attacker doesn't need hacking tools. Sometimes the weakness is simply an unlocked phone.

Fix it now

Do the first three now. Finish the rest at home.

Crack, risk, switch

Why do people use cracked software? It's free, it's easy, everyone does it. Here is what you can't see.

  1. Cracked software
  2. Unknown modification
  3. Possible malware or unwanted software
  4. Credential and session theft
  5. Data loss and privacy loss
  6. Account compromise
You don't know what was modified, what you're installing, or what it is doing.

Think first

Before you click

Four steps for any message, link or request that feels urgent.

  1. STOPDon't react. Scammers want you in a hurry.
  2. CHECKSender, link, urgency, and any request for money or a password.
  3. VERIFYCall or meet the person another way. Never use the link in the message.
  4. ACTReport it, block it, then delete it.

Messages like these are scams

  • "Urgent! I need BDT 3,000. Send it now."
  • "Your university account will be disabled today. Click here immediately."
  • "You won a prize! Share your details to claim it."

Call, don't reply. If a friend asks for money, confirm by phone call first.

Be an upstander

Cyberbullying: check, save, report

Is this cyberbullying? If any of these is true, it is worth acting on.

It is not your fault. The person bullying is responsible, and you do not have to handle it alone.

If it happens to you

  1. STOPDon't reply, don't pay, don't meet. Don't delete anything yet.
  2. SAVEScreenshot with the date, username and link. Save the chat and profile URL. Note the times.
  3. SECUREBlock the account, change passwords, turn on 2FA, make your profile private, log out of other devices.
  4. REPORTPlatform, then campus, then police or a helpline (next section). Tell someone you trust.

If you see it happen to someone else

  • Don't share it, don't comment, don't laugh.
  • Save evidence and support them privately.
  • Report the post or account, and help them use the routes below.

What would you do?

Ten real situations. Tap an answer to see the best choice. Nothing is recorded.

Answered: 0 of 10

PrivacyCard 1

A stranger sends you a friend request. You share 3 mutual friends.

Best answer: C. Check their profile and ask a mutual friend first

Why. Fake accounts add real friends to look genuine. Check how old the profile is and what it posts, and ask first.

Watch out. B feels safe, but by then they can already see your posts.

PrivacyCard 2

A photo-editing app asks for your camera, contacts and location.

Best answer: B. Allow only what editing needs

Why. An app should get only what it needs to do its job. A photo editor does not need your contacts or location.

Watch out. C: "later" rarely comes.

PrivacyCard 3

A friend posts a photo of you that you don't like.

Best answer: B. Ask them to remove it; untag or report if they refuse

Why. Start with a calm request. You can untag yourself and report the post if it is not removed.

Watch out. D: anger in public makes it spread.

DeviceCard 4

Your phone has said "Update available" for 3 weeks.

Best answer: B. Update it: updates fix known security holes

Why. Attackers use known holes in old versions. Updating closes them.

Watch out. A: "works fine" means you can't see the problem.

DeviceCard 5

You lose your phone and it has no screen lock.

Best answer: B. Lock it with Find My Device, change passwords from another device

Why. Lock or erase it remotely, then change your email and social passwords. Add a screen lock to your next phone.

Watch out. D: blocking the SIM does not protect your apps and accounts.

DeviceCard 6

A friend's WhatsApp says: "Urgent! I need BDT 3,000. Send it now."

Best answer: C. Call your friend another way to confirm

Why. Their account may be hacked, and a reply goes to the attacker. A call confirms who it really is.

Watch out. B: replying keeps you talking to the attacker.

SoftwareCard 7

A friend gives you a "pre-activated" version of expensive software.

Best answer: C. Look for a legitimate or free alternative

Why. You can't know what was changed inside it. A free or student version is safer.

Watch out. B: switching off protection is often the first step of an attack.

SoftwareCard 8

A download site tells you to disable your antivirus before installing.

Best answer: B. Stop. That is a red flag

Why. Legitimate software never needs your protection switched off.

Watch out. C: five minutes is enough for malware to settle in.

UpstanderCard 9

An edited photo of a classmate is going around a group chat.

Best answer: B. Don't share; save evidence, support them and report

Why. Sharing makes you part of it. Saving evidence and supporting them helps, and reporting can stop it.

Watch out. D: a laugh still spreads it and hurts them.

UpstanderCard 10

A fake account is using your friend's name and photo.

Best answer: C. Tell your friend, report the account and use the report routes

Why. Your friend needs to know. Reports from several people can speed up removal.

Watch out. D: threatening back can make things worse and put you at risk.

Why it matters

About Jagoron and SDG 16

Jagoron means awakening. A safe campus online is a peaceful and just campus: students know their rights, protect their privacy, and know where to report.

SDG16

Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions. Jagoron supports targets on reducing violence and harassment (16.1), protecting children from abuse and exploitation (16.2), access to justice (16.3), accountable institutions (16.6), inclusive and participatory decisions (16.7), and access to information and protection of fundamental freedoms (16.10).
